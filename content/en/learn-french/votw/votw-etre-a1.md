# Être

*Être* is the French verb you meet first and keep meeting. The closest English meaning is "to be," and that match holds for much of the French you will encounter early: who someone is, where they are, and how they are.

The hard part is not the idea of *être*. It is noticing where French chooses a different verb than English would. You say "I am 20," "I am hungry," or "it is sunny." French often uses *avoir* or *faire* instead.

Start with the everyday uses of *être*. Then notice the places where another verb takes over. That split is one of the most useful early skills you can build.

## Forms and grammar

*Être* is irregular, and every present form is worth learning early because so many of your sentences will depend on it:

<!-- table: forms -->

| Singular | Plural |
|----------|--------|
| je suis | nous sommes |
| tu es | vous êtes |
| il / elle / on est | ils / elles sont |

Notice how little the forms share with the infinitive *être*. *Je suis* and *nous sommes* do not look like the dictionary form at all, so treat the grid as a set to learn. Practice each form with its subject pronoun (*je*, *tu*, *il* / *elle*, *nous*, *vous*, *ils* / *elles*).

In the past tense, *être* itself uses *avoir*, and the participle is *été*:

| French | English |
|--------|---------|
| J'ai été malade hier. | I was sick yesterday. |
| Elle a été très claire. | She was very clear. |

That is different from the group of motion verbs that *use* *être* as their past helper (*je suis allé*, *elle est arrivée*). Same word, two jobs. You will meet that second job later.

## How to use être

The uses below are sorted by the question you are answering with *être*: who or what someone is, what they do for work, where they are, and how they are.

### Saying who or what someone is

To name a person, a nationality, or a basic identity.

| French | English |
|--------|---------|
| Je suis Marie. | I am Marie. |
| Nous sommes canadiens. | We are Canadian. |

For pointing something out, French often prefers *c'est* over *il est* / *elle est*.

| French | English |
|--------|---------|
| C'est mon frère. | This is my brother. / That's my brother. |
| C'est un bon restaurant. | It's a good restaurant. |

### Naming a profession

To say what someone does for work. French has two common shapes, and English "he is a doctor" does not tell you which one to use.

| French | English |
|--------|---------|
| Il est médecin. | He is a doctor. |
| Elle est étudiante. | She is a student. |
| Je suis professeur. | I am a teacher. |

With *il est* / *elle est* / *je suis*, French often drops the article before the job word.

*C'est un / une* + job is also correct, but it tends to introduce or identify someone, not just state their role.

| French | English |
|--------|---------|
| C'est un médecin. | He's a doctor. / That's a doctor. |
| C'est une étudiante. | She's a student. / That's a student. |

*Il est médecin* presents the profession as a fact about the person. *C'est un médecin* can introduce or identify someone as a doctor. Early on, learn both shapes from examples. Do not force every English "he is" through one French phrase.

### Saying where someone or something is

To locate a person or thing in a place.

| French | English |
|--------|---------|
| Je suis à la maison. | I am at home. |
| Le livre est sur la table. | The book is on the table. |
| Nous sommes à Paris. | We are in Paris. |

With cities, French uses *à*. With most feminine countries, it uses *en* (*en France*). With many masculine countries, it uses *au* (*au Canada*). Start with a few places you actually talk about, then expand the list.

### Describing someone or something

To say how a person or thing is, often with an adjective.

| French | English |
|--------|---------|
| Je suis fatigué. | I am tired. |
| La chambre est grande. | The room is big. |
| Ils sont prêts. | They are ready. |

Adjectives usually agree with the person or thing described: *fatigué* / *fatiguée*, *prêt* / *prête*. At [A1](/en/cefr/), notice the agreement in the examples you meet, even before you can explain every ending.

## Other common constructions

Here are a few high-frequency lines that use *être* outside the senses above:

| French | English |
|--------|---------|
| C'est lundi. | It's Monday. |
| C'est à toi. | It's your turn. / It's yours. |
| Je suis en train de lire. | I am reading (right now). |

*Être en train de* + infinitive lets you stress that something is happening right now. It is stronger than the plain present *je lis*, which can mean "I read" or "I am reading."

## Expressions and idioms

These are fixed phrases. Learn each one as a whole unit rather than word by word.

### être d'accord

To agree.

| French | English |
|--------|---------|
| Je suis d'accord. | I agree. |
| Tu es d'accord ? | Do you agree? |

### être en retard / être à l'heure

To be late / to be on time.

| French | English |
|--------|---------|
| Je suis en retard. | I am late. |
| Elle est à l'heure. | She is on time. |

### Ça y est

To say that something is done, ready, or finally happening.

| French | English |
|--------|---------|
| Ça y est, on peut partir. | That's it, we can go. / Ready, we can go. |

## Common mistakes

The mistakes below share the same slip: translating English *be* straight into *être* when French uses another verb or another shape.

### Using *être* for age

French uses *avoir* for age. You "have" the years. You do not "be" them.

| Incorrect | Correct |
|-----------|---------|
| *Je suis 20 ans.* | *J'ai 20 ans.* |

### Using *être* for hunger, thirst, and similar states

Many common physical states use *avoir* + a noun: *faim*, *soif*, *froid*, *chaud*, *peur*.

| Incorrect | Correct |
|-----------|---------|
| *Je suis faim.* | *J'ai faim.* |
| *Elle est soif.* | *Elle a soif.* |

### Forcing *il est* where French uses *c'est*

When you introduce or identify something, *c'est* is often the natural choice.

| Incorrect | Correct |
|-----------|---------|
| *Il est mon ami.* | *C'est mon ami.* |
| *Elle est ma sœur.* | *C'est ma sœur.* |

### Using *il est* for "there is"

Existence and presence usually open with *il y a*, not *il est*. For more on *il y a*, see [Avoir: Everyday Uses](/en/learn-french/votw/votw-avoir-a1/).

| Incorrect | Correct |
|-----------|---------|
| *Il est un problème.* | *Il y a un problème.* |
| *Il est beaucoup de monde.* | *Il y a beaucoup de monde.* |

## Related verbs

- [avoir](/en/learn-french/votw/votw-avoir-a1/): to have (age, possession, many body states)
- aller: to go
- devenir: to become
- sembler: to seem

> *Avoir* is the one to watch. English *be* often maps to *avoir* in French, especially with age and feelings like hunger or cold. See [Avoir: Everyday Uses](/en/learn-french/votw/votw-avoir-a1/) for that side of the pair:

| French | English |
|--------|---------|
| J'ai 30 ans. | I am 30. |
| J'ai froid. | I am cold. |
| J'ai peur. | I am scared. |

## Before you go

Use *être* with confidence for identity, profession, place, and description. At the same time, watch for the English *be* lines that French sends to *avoir* or elsewhere. Keep both in view and you will cover a lot of [A1](/en/cefr/) French.

For the *avoir* side of that split, continue with [Avoir: Everyday Uses](/en/learn-french/votw/votw-avoir-a1/). When you are ready for past tense helpers, see [Passé composé with avoir and être](/en/learn-french/articles/passe-compose-avoir-etre/).

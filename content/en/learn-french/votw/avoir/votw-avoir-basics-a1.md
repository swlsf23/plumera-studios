# How to use avoir

Like *être*, *avoir* is one of the most important French verbs. The closest English meaning is "to have," and that match is solid when expressing possession of something. 

The surprise for English speakers is how often French uses *avoir* where English uses *be*.

You have years, hunger, thirst, cold, and fear. French packages those ideas with a noun after *avoir*:

<!-- pattern -->
*avoir* + noun

Here are some of the most common ones:

| French | English |
|--------|---------|
| J'ai 20 ans. | I am 20. |
| J'ai faim. | I am hungry. |
| J'ai soif. | I am thirsty. |
| J'ai froid. | I am cold. |
| J'ai peur. | I am scared. |

You will also meet *avoir* early as the usual helper in the past tense:

<!-- pattern -->
*avoir* + past participle

Here are some common examples:

| French | English |
|--------|---------|
| J'ai mangé. | I ate. / I have eaten. |
| Elle a parlé. | She spoke. / She has spoken. |

## Forms and grammar

*Avoir* is irregular, and the present forms are worth learning early because they support a lot of basic French:

<!-- table: forms -->

| Singular | Plural |
|----------|--------|
| j'ai | nous avons |
| tu as | vous avez |
| il / elle / on a | ils / elles ont |

Notice the spelling splits: *ai*, *as*, *a*, then *avons*, *avez*, *ont*. Practice each form with its subject pronoun (*je* becomes *j'* before *ai*).

In the past tense, *avoir* is the most common helper:

<!-- pattern -->
*avoir* + past participle

Some verbs use *être* instead. For that split, see [Passé composé with avoir and être](/en/learn-french/articles/passe-compose-avoir-etre/).

## How to use avoir

The uses of *avoir* below are sorted by what it is expressing: holding something, saying something exists, expressing age, or naming a common state.

### Having or owning something

Use *avoir* to say that something belongs to you, or that you have it with you.

| French | English |
|--------|---------|
| J'ai un livre. | I have a book. |
| Tu as mon numéro ? | Do you have my number? |
| Nous avons une voiture. | We have a car. |

Keep the article. French says *j'ai un livre*, not *j'ai livre*. For more than one, use *des*:

| French | English |
|--------|---------|
| J'ai un livre. | I have a book. |
| J'ai des livres. | I have (some) books. |

> *Des* is the plural partner of *un / une*. English can drop "some." French usually keeps the determiner.

### There is / there are: *il y a*

*Il y a* is how French says "there is / there are." Learn it as a fixed opener.

| French | English |
|--------|---------|
| Il y a un café ici. | There is a café here. |
| Il y a beaucoup de monde. | There are a lot of people. |

The *a* in these phrases comes from *avoir*, but you do not conjugate the phrase like an ordinary *avoir* sentence. 

> *Il est un café ici* is incorrect.

### Saying how old someone is

Use *avoir* to express age. French expresses your age as something you have.

| French | English |
|--------|---------|
| J'ai 20 ans. | I am 20. |
| Elle a 8 ans. | She is 8. |
| Quel âge as-tu ? | How old are you? |

> Always include *ans*. *J'ai 20* is incomplete.

### Expressing common states

Use *avoir* with a noun for hunger, thirst, cold, heat, fear, and similar states. Many English *be* expressions use *avoir* here.

<!-- pattern -->
*avoir* + noun

Learn each line as a whole phrase (*avoir faim*, *avoir soif*, and the rest).

| French | English |
|--------|---------|
| J'ai faim. | I am hungry. |
| Tu as soif ? | Are you thirsty? |
| Il a froid. | He is cold. |
| J'ai chaud. | I am hot / warm. |
| J'ai peur. | I am scared. |

## Before you go

Ask what idea you are expressing, not whether English uses *be*. Possession, age, common states, and *il y a* usually take *avoir*. Keep those patterns in view, and you will cover a large part of [A1](/en/cefr/) French.

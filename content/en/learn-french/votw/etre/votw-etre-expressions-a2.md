# Expressions and common errors with *être*

After you've learned the basic uses of *être*, the next step is learning common expressions and recognizing when English *be* translates to another French verb. For the core uses, see [How to use *être*](/en/learn-french/votw/etre/votw-etre-basics-a1/).

## Common expressions

These are fixed phrases. Learn each one as a whole unit rather than word by word.

### être d'accord

Use *être d'accord* to say that you agree with someone.

| French | English |
|--------|---------|
| Je suis d'accord. | I agree. |
| Tu es d'accord ? | Do you agree? |

### être en retard / être à l'heure

Use *être en retard* and *être à l'heure* to say that someone is late or on time.

| French | English |
|--------|---------|
| Je suis en retard. | I am late. |
| Elle est à l'heure. | She is on time. |

### Ça y est

Use *Ça y est* when something is done, ready, or finally happening.

| French | English |
|--------|---------|
| Ça y est, on peut partir. | That's it, we can go. / Ready, we can go. |
| Ça y est ! J'ai trouvé mes clés. | That's it! I found my keys. |
| Ça y est, c'est fini. | That's it, it's finished. |

### être en train de

Use *être en train de* with an infinitive to emphasize that something is happening right now.

<!-- pattern -->
*être en train de* + infinitive

Here are some common examples:

| French | English |
|--------|---------|
| Je suis en train de lire. | I am reading (right now). |
| Nous sommes en train de manger. | We are eating (right now). |

That shape is stronger than the plain present *je lis*, which can mean "I read" or "I am reading."

## Common errors

The mistakes below share the same type of error by translating English *be* straight into *être* when French uses another verb or another shape.

### Using *être* for age

French uses *avoir* for age. You "have" the years. You do not "be" them.

| Incorrect | Correct |
|-----------|---------|
| *Je suis 20 ans.* | *J'ai 20 ans.* |

### Using *être* for hunger, thirst, and similar states

Many common physical states use *avoir* with a noun: *faim*, *soif*, *froid*, *chaud*, *peur*.

<!-- pattern -->
*avoir* + noun

Watch for slips like these:

| Incorrect | Correct |
|-----------|---------|
| *Je suis faim.* | *J'ai faim.* |
| *Elle est soif.* | *Elle a soif.* |

### Forcing *il est* where French uses *c'est*

When you introduce or identify someone or something, *c'est* is usually the natural choice.

*Elle est ma sœur* can be fine if *elle* already refers to a person in the conversation. The slip is using *il est* / *elle est* to present or identify someone for the first time. A natural pattern is *c'est* first, then *elle* in the next sentence:

| French | English |
|--------|---------|
| C'est Marie. Elle est ma sœur. | This is Marie. She is my sister. |

The following examples demonstrate a common error when *c'est* should introduce or identify:

| Incorrect | Correct |
|-----------|---------|
| *Il est mon professeur.* | *C'est mon professeur.* |
| *Elle est une amie.* | *C'est une amie.* |

### Using *il est* for "there is"

To express existence and presence, French usually starts with *il y a*, not *il est*. For more on *il y a*, see [How to use *avoir*](/en/learn-french/votw/avoir/votw-avoir-basics-a1/).

| Incorrect | Correct |
|-----------|---------|
| *Il est un problème.* | *Il y a un problème.* |
| *Il est beaucoup de monde.* | *Il y a beaucoup de monde.* |

## Before you go

Treat the phrases above as whole units, and keep the differences between *être* and *avoir* in mind in situations where English expresses *be*. For the core uses of *être*, take a look at [How to use *être*](/en/learn-french/votw/etre/votw-etre-basics-a1/). To understand *avoir*, see [How to use *avoir*](/en/learn-french/votw/avoir/votw-avoir-basics-a1/).

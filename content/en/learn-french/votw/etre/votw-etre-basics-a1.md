# Être - basics

*Être* is usually the first French verb you learn, and one you'll use constantly. Like English *to be*, it expresses identity, location, and description.

One early challenge is that French does not always use *être* where English uses "to be." For age, hunger, and many everyday expressions, French uses other verbs like *avoir* or *faire* instead.

Start by learning the core uses of *être*. Then you will be able to recognize when another French verb is needed.

## Forms and grammar

*Être* is irregular, and you must learn these forms early because many basic sentences depend on it:

<!-- table: forms -->

| Singular | Plural |
|----------|--------|
| je suis | nous sommes |
| tu es | vous êtes |
| il / elle / on est | ils / elles sont |

Notice how little the forms share with the infinitive *être*. *Je suis* and *nous sommes* do not look like the infinitive form at all, so learn these forms as a set. Practice each form with its subject pronoun.

When *être* is the main verb in the passé composé, it uses *avoir* as its helper. The past participle is *été*:

<!-- pattern -->
*avoir* + *été*

Here is how that looks in a full sentence:

| French | English |
|--------|---------|
| J'ai été malade hier. | I was sick yesterday. |
| Elle a été très claire. | She was very clear. |

For uses of *être* as a past-tense helper with other verbs, see [Passé composé with avoir and être](/en/learn-french/articles/passe-compose-avoir-etre/):

<!-- pattern -->
*être* + past participle

## How to use être

The uses below cover some of the most common examples of *être*.

### Saying your name or nationality

Use *être* to say your name, your nationality, or basic information about yourself.

| French | English |
|--------|---------|
| Je suis Marie. | I am Marie. |
| Il est français. | He is French. |
| Nous sommes canadiens. | We are Canadians. |
| Elle est ma sœur. | She is my sister. |

When introducing or identifying someone or something, French often uses *c'est* instead of *il est* or *elle est*.

| French | English |
|--------|---------|
| C'est mon frère. | This is my brother. |
| C'est un bon restaurant. | It is a good restaurant. |

### Talking about location

*Être* is used for the location of people and things.

| French | English |
|--------|---------|
| Je suis à la maison. | I am at home. |
| Le livre est sur la table. | The book is on the table. |
| Nous sommes à Paris. | We are in Paris. |

With cities, French uses *à*. With most feminine countries, it uses *en* (*en France*). With many masculine countries, it uses *au* (*au Canada*). 

Start with a few places you actually talk about, then expand the list.

### Describing someone or something

French uses *être* to describe someone or something, often with an adjective.

| French | English |
|--------|---------|
| Je suis fatigué. | I am tired. |
| La chambre est grande. | The room is big. |
| Ils sont prêts. | They are ready. |

The adjective changes to match the gender and number of the person or thing it describes: *fatigué* / *fatiguée*, *prêt* / *prête*. At [A1](/en/cefr/), notice the agreement in the examples you meet, even before you can explain every ending.

### Talking about professions

French usually omits the article after *être* when simply stating someone's profession.

| French | English |
|--------|---------|
| Il est médecin. | He is a doctor. |
| Elle est étudiante. | She is a student. |
| Je suis professeur. | I am a teacher. |

The identification pattern tends to introduce or identify someone, not just state their role:

<!-- pattern -->
*c'est un / une* + job

These identification lines follow that shape:

| French | English |
|--------|---------|
| C'est un médecin. | He's a doctor. / That's a doctor. |
| C'est une étudiante. | She's a student. / That's a student. |

*Il est médecin* presents the profession as a fact about the person. *C'est un médecin* can introduce or identify someone as a doctor. Learn both shapes from examples. 

> Do not force every English "he is" through one French phrase.

## Useful expressions using *c'est*

Here are a few high-frequency lines that use *être* outside the uses described above:

| French | English |
|--------|---------|
| C'est lundi. | It's Monday. |
| C'est à toi. | It's your turn. / It's yours. |
| C'est vrai. | That's true. |
| C'est facile. | It's easy. |

## Related verbs

- [avoir](/en/learn-french/votw/avoir/votw-avoir-basics-a1/): to have (age, possession, many body states)
- aller: to go
- devenir: to become
- sembler: to seem

> *Avoir* is the one to watch. English *be* often maps to *avoir* in French, especially with age and feelings like hunger or cold. See [Avoir - basics](/en/learn-french/votw/avoir/votw-avoir-basics-a1/).

## Before you go

Use *être* when you say who someone is, what they do, where they are, or how they are. Do not assume every context where English uses *be* maps to *être* in French. Age, hunger, and similar states usually take *avoir*. 

Remember these differences, and you'll understand *être* through most of [A1](/en/cefr/) French.

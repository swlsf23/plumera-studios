# Idioms and fixed expressions with *laisser*

At [A2](/en/cefr/), *laisser* covers leaving things behind, letting someone do something, and leaving something in a state. That core is covered in the [French Verb of the Week lesson on *laisser*](/en/learn-french/votw/votw-laisser-a2/).

Around [B1](/en/cefr/), you meet pronominal forms and fixed phrases where the whole construction carries the meaning. "Leave" and "let" still help a little, but less than before.

## Letting yourself: *se laisser*

*Se laisser* means allowing something to happen to yourself, either deliberately or passively.

| French | English |
|--------|---------|
| Elle s'est laissée convaincre. | She let herself be convinced. |
| Ne te laisse pas décourager. | Don't let yourself get discouraged. |
| Il se laisse facilement influencer. | He lets himself be influenced easily. |

English often changes the verb (*get*, *be*). French keeps *se laisser* + infinitive.

### se laisser faire

A denser related phrase: letting yourself be pushed around, or not resisting.

| French | English |
|--------|---------|
| Il se laisse trop faire. | He lets himself be pushed around too much. |
| Ne te laisse pas faire ! | Don't let them walk all over you! |

## Letting time and situations unfold

*Laisser* + infinitive can also mean allowing events to unfold without interfering. This is close to the A2 "let" sense, but the subject is often time, a discussion, or a situation rather than a person you permit.

| French | English |
|--------|---------|
| Laisse passer quelques jours. | Let a few days go by. |
| Il a laissé tomber la discussion. | He dropped the discussion. |
| Laissons faire le temps. | Let's let time do its work. |

At [A2](/en/cefr/), *laisser tomber* already appears as "forget it / drop it." Here it often means abandoning a topic or plan.

## Implying or suggesting

In careful speech and writing, *laisser* often introduces what something suggests without stating it outright.

| French | English |
|--------|---------|
| Il a laissé entendre que le projet allait changer. | He implied that the project was going to change. |
| Cette décision laisse penser que la règle a changé. | This decision suggests that the rule has changed. |
| Son attitude laisse croire qu'il est prêt. | His attitude suggests that he's ready. |

English prefers *imply*, *suggest*, or *make someone think*. French keeps *laisser* + verb of thinking or understanding.

## Impressions and standards

Two more fixed phrases are useful in everyday evaluation.

| French | English |
|--------|---------|
| Elle a laissé une bonne impression. | She left a good impression. |
| Le service laisse à désirer. | The service leaves a lot to be desired. |

## A pattern worth noticing

Past the everyday [A2](/en/cefr/) meanings, *laisser* often appears in larger constructions:

| French | English |
|--------|---------|
| *se laisser* + infinitive | let yourself (be / get)… |
| *se laisser faire* | let yourself be pushed around |
| *laisser tomber* | drop / give up on |
| *laisser entendre que* | imply that |
| *laisser penser que* | suggest that |
| *laisser à désirer* | leave something to be desired |

Learning these as complete patterns is more useful than searching for another translation of *laisser*.

## Summary

Past the core meanings of leaving and allowing, *laisser* becomes a verb of attitude, implication, and passive permission. Learning those expressions as units will make them easier to recognize in authentic French at [B1](/en/cefr/).

# Faire - basics

*Faire* can mean "to do" or "to make" in English. But you’ll use it for much more than that.

English splits those uses across *do*, *make*, *be*, *go*, and *pay*. French often keeps *faire*.

After you recognize these patterns, combinations like *faire beau*, *faire du vélo*, and *faire attention* start to make more sense.

## Forms and grammar

*Faire* is irregular in the present tense. The singular forms are similar, but pay attention to the plural forms:

<!-- table: forms -->

| Singular | Plural |
|----------|--------|
| je fais | nous faisons |
| tu fais | vous faites |
| il / elle fait | ils / elles font |

Notice *vous faites*. Unlike most French verbs, *faire* has a *vous* form ending in **-tes**. You’ll also need to remember *ils font*, which looks very different from the infinitive.

To use *faire* in the passé composé, combine *avoir* with the past participle *fait*:

| French | English |
|--------|---------|
| J'ai fait un gâteau. | I made a cake. |
| Nous avons fait du sport. | We exercised. |

## How to use faire

You’ll use *faire* in several everyday situations, from doing or making something to talking about the weather and activities.

### Doing or making

You can use *faire* for actions you carry out and things you create:

| French                  | English                |
| ----------------------- | ---------------------- |
| Je fais mes devoirs.    | I'm doing my homework. |
| Elle fait un gâteau.    | She's making a cake.   |
| Nous faisons une liste. | We're making a list.   |

English chooses *do* or *make*. French keeps *faire* for both.

### Talking about the weather

You’ll use *faire* in many common weather expressions, where English usually uses *be*:

| French                     | English              |
| -------------------------- | -------------------- |
| Il fait beau.              | The weather is nice. |
| Il fait froid.             | It's cold.           |
| Il fait chaud aujourd'hui. | It's hot today.      |

### Doing activities

You’ll also use *faire* for many sports, errands, and everyday activities where English prefers *go*, *do*, or a more specific verb:

| French                 | English                        |
| ---------------------- | ------------------------------ |
| Je fais du vélo.       | I go cycling. / I bike.        |
| Nous faisons du sport. | We exercise. / We play sports. |
| Elle fait les courses. | She's doing the shopping.      |

## Related verbs

| Verb | English | When to use it |
| ---- | ------- | -------------- |
| *fabriquer* | to manufacture or build | When you make or manufacture something from materials |
| *créer* | to create | When the idea of creating something new matters |
| *réaliser* | to carry out or achieve | When you carry out or complete a project, plan, or piece of work |

*Fabriquer* is more specific than *faire*. You use *fabriquer* for something you manufacture or build, such as a table or a piece of furniture. You use *faire* for broader everyday actions, such as making a cake, doing your homework, or doing the shopping.

## Before you go

As you recognize more of these patterns, you'll rely less on translating *faire* word by word. This becomes important with more advanced uses, where a direct English translation won't always help.

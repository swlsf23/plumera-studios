# Avoir

*Avoir* is the other French verb pillar beside *être*. The closest English meaning is "to have," and that match is solid when expressing possession of something. The surprise for English speakers is how often French uses *avoir* where English uses *be*.

You have years, hunger, cold, and fear. French packages those ideas with *avoir* + a noun: *j'ai 20 ans*, *j'ai faim*, *j'ai froid*, *j'ai peur*.

Learn *avoir* for what you own, how old you are, and how you feel in those fixed lines. You will also meet it early as the usual helper in the past tense (*j'ai mangé*).

## Forms and grammar

*Avoir* is irregular, and the present forms are worth learning early because they support so much basic French:

<!-- table: forms -->

| Singular | Plural |
|----------|--------|
| j'ai | nous avons |
| tu as | vous avez |
| il / elle / on a | ils / elles ont |

Notice the spelling splits: *ai*, *as*, *a*, then *avons*, *avez*, *ont*. Practice each form with its subject pronoun (*je* becomes *j'* before *ai*).

In the past tense, *avoir* is also the usual helper verb. The participle of *avoir* itself is *eu*:

| French | English |
|--------|---------|
| J'ai eu une idée. | I had an idea. |
| Elle a eu de la chance. | She was lucky. / She had some luck. |

When you talk about the past, French often builds the sentence with a form of *avoir* plus a past participle: *j'ai mangé*, *tu as parlé*, *nous avons pris le bus*. A smaller group of motion verbs uses *être* instead. For both helpers, see [Passé composé with avoir and être](/en/learn-french/articles/passe-compose-avoir-etre/).

## How to use avoir

The uses below are sorted by what *avoir* is expressing: holding something, counting years, or naming a common state.

### Having or owning something

To say that something belongs to you, or that you have it with you.

| French | English |
|--------|---------|
| J'ai un livre. | I have a book. |
| Tu as mon numéro ? | Do you have my number? |
| Nous avons une voiture. | We have a car. |

Keep the article. French says *j'ai un livre*, not *j'ai livre*. For more than one, use *des*:

| French | English |
|--------|---------|
| J'ai un livre. | I have a book. |
| J'ai des livres. | I have (some) books. |

*Des* is the plural partner of *un / une*. English can drop "some." French usually keeps the determiner.

### Saying how old someone is

To express your age, French counts the years as something you have.

| French | English |
|--------|---------|
| J'ai 20 ans. | I am 20. |
| Elle a 8 ans. | She is 8. |
| Quel âge as-tu ? | How old are you? |

The word *ans* matters. *J'ai 20* alone does not work in French.

### Expressing common states

To talk about hunger, thirst, cold, heat, fear, and similar states with *avoir* + a noun. English uses *be* here. French uses *avoir*. Learn each line as a whole phrase (*avoir faim*, *avoir soif*, and the rest).

| French | English |
|--------|---------|
| J'ai faim. | I am hungry. |
| Tu as soif ? | Are you thirsty? |
| Il a froid. | He is cold. |
| J'ai chaud. | I am hot / warm. |
| J'ai peur. | I am scared. |

## Other common constructions

One high-frequency shape sits just outside the senses above: *il y a*.

| French | English |
|--------|---------|
| Il y a un café ici. | There is a café here. |
| Il y a beaucoup de monde. | There are a lot of people. |

*Il y a* is how French says "there is / there are." Learn it as a fixed opener. The *a* is from *avoir*, but you do not conjugate the phrase like an ordinary *avoir* sentence. *Il est un café ici* is the wrong track.

## Expressions and idioms

These are fixed phrases. Learn each one as a whole unit rather than word by word.

### avoir raison / avoir tort

To be right / to be wrong.

| French | English |
|--------|---------|
| Tu as raison. | You are right. |
| Tu as tort. | You are wrong. |

### avoir envie de

To want something, or to feel like doing something.

| French | English |
|--------|---------|
| J'ai envie d'un café. | I feel like a coffee. / I want a coffee. |
| Tu as envie de sortir ? | Do you feel like going out? |

### avoir besoin de

To need something, or to need to do something.

| French | English |
|--------|---------|
| J'ai besoin d'aide. | I need help. |
| Tu as besoin de dormir. | You need to sleep. |

### avoir de la chance

To be lucky.

| French | English |
|--------|---------|
| Tu as de la chance. | You are lucky. |

## Common mistakes

The mistakes below share the same slip: using *être* (or another English-shaped line) where French wants *avoir*.

### Using *être* for age

To express your age, use *avoir*. Do not use être.

| Incorrect | Correct |
|-----------|---------|
| *Je suis 20 ans.* | *J'ai 20 ans.* |
| *Je suis 20.* | *J'ai 20 ans.* |

### Using *être* for hunger, thirst, and similar states

These states are nouns after *avoir*, not adjectives after *être*.

| Incorrect | Correct |
|-----------|---------|
| *Je suis faim.* | *J'ai faim.* |
| *Elle est soif.* | *Elle a soif.* |
| *Je suis froid.* | *J'ai froid.* |

*Je suis froid* can mean you come across as distant or unfriendly. For body temperature, use *j'ai froid*.

## Related verbs

- être: to be (identity, place, description)
- prendre: to take / to have food or drink in many café lines
- vouloir: to want
- devoir: to have to / must

> *Être* is the one to watch. Many English *be* lines belong to *avoir* in French. For the core *être* uses, see [Être: Everyday Uses](/en/learn-french/votw/votw-etre-a1/):

| French | English |
|--------|---------|
| Je suis fatigué. | I am tired. |
| J'ai faim. | I am hungry. |
| Je suis à la maison. | I am at home. |
| J'ai 30 ans. | I am 30. |

## Before you go

Use *avoir* for possession, age, and the common *avoir* + noun states. Add *il y a* for "there is," and expect *avoir* again as the usual past-tense helper. Together with [être](/en/learn-french/votw/votw-etre-a1/), that covers a large share of [A1](/en/cefr/) French.

When you are ready to sort past-tense helpers in one place, continue with [Passé composé with avoir and être](/en/learn-french/articles/passe-compose-avoir-etre/).
